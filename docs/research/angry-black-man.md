# The Angry Black Man

## How a Racialized Threat Trope Shapes Perception, Punishment, Work, and Well-Being in the United States

**Author:** DJ Martin  
**Manuscript type:** Integrative scholarly review and conceptual paper  
**Version:** 1.0 — pre-submission manuscript  
**Date:** September 17, 2026  

> **Status note:** This is a scholarly working paper prepared for public review and later journal adaptation. It synthesizes existing empirical literature and proposes a conceptual framework. It does **not** report original human-subjects research, and it should not be represented as peer reviewed unless and until it completes peer review.

## Abstract

The “Angry Black Man” is a familiar American cultural trope, but the scientific literature rarely treats it as one discrete construct. Instead, its component parts appear across several research traditions: Black boys are perceived as older and less innocent than White boys; Black male faces are more readily read as angry or threatening under conditions of racial bias; Black men are perceived as larger, stronger, and more physically formidable than equivalently sized White men; anger expressed by Black people can reduce social influence where equivalent anger by White people does not; and racial discrimination is associated with poorer mental-health outcomes among Black men. These findings suggest that the trope is better understood as a **racialized anger-threat schema** than as a simple belief that Black men are frequently angry.

This paper integrates evidence from social psychology, education, employment, policing, and health research to propose a life-course model of that schema. The model distinguishes five linked processes: **presumption**, in which anger or dangerousness is inferred before clear evidence exists; **amplification**, in which ambiguous conduct is interpreted as more hostile or threatening; **penalty**, in which the perceived threat changes institutional or interpersonal responses; **adaptation**, in which Black men monitor, suppress, soften, or strategically perform emotion in response to anticipated misinterpretation; and **accumulation**, in which repeated encounters create social, economic, and psychological costs over time. The paper argues that the distinctive burden of the trope is a double bind: Black men can be treated as angry when neutral and punished as threatening when genuinely angry, leaving a narrowed range of socially “safe” emotional expression.

The review also emphasizes limits. Black men are not a monolith; racialized perception varies across settings, observers, age, class, phenotype, and other identities. The evidence does not justify attributing every racial disparity to a single stereotype, nor does the existence of stereotype effects imply that every observer or institution behaves identically. The paper concludes with a research agenda and institutional design principles centered on behaviorally specific evaluation, structured decision rules, outcome auditing, and safeguards that reduce the power of ambiguous impressions to become consequential judgments.

**Keywords:** Black men; anger; threat perception; racial stereotypes; adultification; emotion; discrimination; policing; workplace bias; mental health

## 1. Introduction

American culture contains a recurring image of the Black man as volatile, dangerous, physically imposing, criminal, or one provocation away from violence. Popular language often compresses these ideas into the figure of the **“Angry Black Man.”** Yet the most important feature of the trope is not anger alone. It is the conversion of Black male emotion, physical presence, disagreement, confidence, frustration, and even neutrality into evidence of threat.

That distinction matters. A stereotype does not need to be verbally announced in order to shape perception. It can operate upstream of conscious judgment by changing what an observer believes they saw, how ambiguous behavior is categorized, how much danger is inferred, or how much force, discipline, exclusion, or social distance appears justified. Research on facial perception, racialized threat, adultification, physical-formidability judgments, workplace influence, and discrimination provides evidence for several pieces of this process even when researchers use different terminology.

The purpose of this paper is therefore not to claim that psychology has already established a single, universally accepted “Angry Black Man effect.” It has not. The purpose is to synthesize adjacent findings that converge on a common social mechanism: **Black men can encounter a lower threshold for being perceived as angry, hostile, formidable, dangerous, or culpable, and those perceptions can change how other people respond to them.**

This is a life-course issue. Black boys first encounter racialized interpretations while still children. In school, ambiguous behavior can be interpreted through expectations of hostility. In adulthood, physical presence may be read as greater threat, anger may carry different social consequences, and labor-market discrimination can constrain opportunity even among equally qualified men. In policing and other security contexts, threat judgments become especially consequential. In healthcare and daily life, related beliefs about physical toughness and pain tolerance can affect whether vulnerability is recognized. Repeated discrimination, meanwhile, is associated with depressive symptoms among Black men.

The argument advanced here is that these are not isolated observations. They can be organized into a coherent model of **racialized interpretive compression**: the process by which the broad emotional and behavioral range of a Black man is compressed into a narrower set of threatening meanings.

## 2. Scope, Method, and Terminology

### 2.1 An integrative review, not a new experiment

This manuscript is an **integrative narrative review and conceptual synthesis**. It draws primarily on peer-reviewed empirical studies and meta-analytic evidence from social psychology, sociology, education, health, and criminology. It also uses authoritative institutional summaries where they clarify study design or practical significance.

It is not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Accordingly, the paper does not claim exhaustive retrieval, calculate pooled effect sizes, or apply a preregistered inclusion protocol. A journal submission could strengthen the manuscript by converting this review into a systematic or scoping review with database search strings, screening criteria, a PRISMA flow diagram, formal risk-of-bias assessment, and a reproducible evidence table.

### 2.2 Why use the phrase “Angry Black Man”?

The phrase is used here as a **cultural label for a composite stereotype**, not as a diagnosis, personality category, or claim about Black men’s actual emotional tendencies. The empirical literature more often studies related constructs—anger recognition, threat perception, racial prototypicality, physical formidability, hostility attribution, criminalization, or dehumanization—than the exact phrase itself.

This distinction prevents a common analytical error: treating a stereotype as if it describes the people stereotyped rather than the perceptual and institutional processes imposed on them.

### 2.3 “Black men” is not a homogeneous category

The category “Black men” encompasses substantial variation in age, nationality, ethnicity, class, region, disability, sexuality, skin tone, phenotype, immigration history, religion, and other identities. Many studies cited here focus specifically on Black American men or on U.S. participants evaluating Black male targets. Findings should not automatically be generalized to every Black man or every national context.

## 3. The Trope as a Racialized Anger-Threat Schema

A useful starting point comes from research showing that racial bias can affect the perception of emotion itself. Hugenberg and Bodenhausen (2003) used facial emotion change-detection tasks with European American participants. Higher implicit racial prejudice was associated with a greater readiness to perceive anger in Black faces, but not in comparable White faces. The importance of this finding is temporal: bias can enter the process **before** a later judgment about whether someone’s anger was justified. The observer may already be seeing more anger.

This mechanism is compatible with research on threat perception. Wilson, Hugenberg, and Rule (2017) conducted seven studies on judgments of physical size and formidability. Participants tended to perceive young Black men as larger, stronger, more muscular, and more capable of causing harm than young White men, including when actual body size was controlled. Those distorted formidability judgments were associated with greater justification of hypothetical force.

Taken together, the studies suggest that the “Angry Black Man” trope is not merely the proposition **Black men get angry**. A more precise formulation is:

> **Black male identity can function as a prior that increases the probability that ambiguous emotion, body size, assertiveness, or conflict will be interpreted as threat.**

That prior can operate even when anger is absent. Once actual anger is expressed, the reaction can be different again. Salerno, Peter-Hagene, and Jay (2019) found experimental evidence that African American group members who expressed anger were perceived as less influential than when they made the same arguments without anger; equivalent anger did not reduce White group members’ influence in the same way. The study’s design is especially instructive because the substantive arguments were held constant. The social consequence changed with the perceived race of the person expressing the emotion.

This produces a distinctive double bind:

1. **Neutrality does not guarantee neutrality of interpretation.** A Black man can be perceived as angry or threatening without clearly expressing anger.
2. **Genuine anger can be costly even when substantively justified.** Once anger is expressed, it can become a reason to discount, fear, discipline, or contain the speaker.

The problem is therefore not simply “anger management.” It is a socially unequal **interpretation problem**.

## 4. The Life Course: From Black Boyhood to Black Manhood

### 4.1 Adultification and the erosion of innocence

The trope begins before adulthood. Goff and colleagues (2014) examined whether Black boys receive the same presumption of childhood innocence as White boys. Across laboratory and field-linked studies, Black boys were perceived as older and less innocent than White peers, and the researchers found evidence connecting dehumanizing associations to disparities in police use of force against children.

Adultification matters to an anger-threat framework because childhood normally supplies interpretive protection. A child’s defiance, impulsivity, fear, or frustration is more likely to be understood as developmental behavior when the observer continues to perceive a child. When a Black boy is perceived as older, more responsible, or less innocent, identical conduct can be assigned an adult meaning: intentional disrespect, hostility, dangerousness, or criminality.

The life-course implication is profound. The Black male subject can enter adolescence having already lost some of the social margin for error that childhood ordinarily provides.

### 4.2 Education: when emotion becomes hostility

Research in educational psychology indicates that racialized emotion perception can affect how adults interpret student behavior. Halberstadt and colleagues’ work on preservice teachers reported racialized differences in emotion recognition and hostility attribution, with misbehavior perceived as more hostile for Black than White boys and anger bias stronger toward Black male targets.

The educational concern is not that every teacher consciously stereotypes Black boys. The concern is that **subjective disciplinary categories**—“defiant,” “aggressive,” “disrespectful,” “threatening,” “bad attitude”—depend heavily on human interpretation. When an observer’s threshold for seeing anger or hostility shifts by race, the same rule can generate unequal consequences even without an explicitly racial rule.

This suggests that equity cannot rely solely on good intentions. Institutions must examine the decision architecture itself: Which behaviors are objectively specified? Which categories are impressionistic? What review process exists when subjective labels produce repeated disparities?

---

## 5. The Body as Evidence: Size, Strength, and Threat

One of the most consequential features of the trope is the conversion of physical presence into presumed dangerousness.

Wilson et al. (2017) found that participants overestimated Black men’s physical size and formidability relative to equivalently sized White men. The effect was not only about descriptive estimates such as height or muscularity. Participants also judged Black men as more capable of causing harm, and those formidability judgments were linked to greater justification of force in hypothetical scenarios.

This evidence matters because common explanations after conflict often invoke size: **He was huge. He looked strong. I thought he could overpower me.** If racial perception can inflate apparent formidability, then size is not always a neutral input into threat assessment. It can itself be a biased perceptual output.

The result is a paradox. Physical strength may be culturally rewarded in athletics, labor, or masculine identity, yet the same perceived strength can become evidence used to justify heightened fear or control. A Black man’s body can be read simultaneously as useful and dangerous, admired and surveilled.

This is one reason the Angry Black Man trope cannot be analyzed only as an emotion stereotype. It is also an **embodied threat stereotype**.

## 6. Public Space, Policing, and the Cost of Threat Attribution

Policing is not reducible to stereotype research, and racial disparities in police outcomes have multiple legal, geographic, socioeconomic, institutional, behavioral, and historical contributors. It would be analytically irresponsible to claim that one perceptual bias explains them all.

At the same time, policing is a domain in which threat perception has unusually high stakes. Edwards, Lee, and Esposito (2019), using 2013–2018 police-involved death data, estimated that Black men and boys faced the highest lifetime risk of being killed by police among the racial-sex groups they analyzed—approximately 1 in 1,000 at those observed risk levels. Their demographic analysis did not identify the Angry Black Man stereotype as the singular cause of that disparity. The relevance here is different: **when institutional decisions turn on rapid judgments of danger, evidence that Black men can be perceived as more threatening than equivalent White men becomes materially important.**

The Wilson et al. (2017) findings provide one plausible perceptual pathway. If Black men are seen as larger and more capable of harm, an observer may believe a higher level of force is proportionate even when objective physical characteristics do not support the difference.

A careful interpretation therefore has two parts:

- population-level disparities do not prove a single psychological mechanism; and
- psychological evidence about biased threat perception should not be ignored simply because real-world outcomes have multiple causes.

Good policy analysis must hold both propositions at once.

## 7. Employment: Before Anger and After Anger

The employment literature reveals two distinct problems: racial disadvantage can occur **before any emotional expression at all**, and anger can create an additional penalty once expressed.

Pager, Western, and Bonikowski (2009) conducted an in-person field experiment in the New York City low-wage labor market using matched young men with equivalent résumés and standardized presentation. Black applicants were substantially less likely than equally qualified White applicants to receive a callback or job offer. Because the testers were matched on qualifications and trained for comparable interaction styles, the study demonstrated how racial discrimination can alter opportunity even when the applicant has not provided a legitimate performance-based reason for differential treatment.

This matters for an anger-threat analysis because it establishes the **baseline racial prior**: Black men may already be navigating a labor market in which identical credentials do not reliably produce identical treatment.

Salerno et al. (2019) then show what can happen when emotion enters the interaction. In their group-decision experiment, African American participants expressing anger lost influence relative to otherwise identical no-anger conditions, while anger did not produce the same loss for White participants. The finding suggests that a Black professional confronting a bad decision, unfair treatment, ethical breach, or operational failure may face an additional burden: the argument can become less influential precisely when the emotion communicates that the issue matters.

This creates a professional dilemma familiar to many racialized workers: how direct can one be before “direct” becomes “aggressive”? How firm can one be before “firm” becomes “hostile”? How visibly frustrated can one be before the substantive issue is displaced by a discussion of tone?

The empirical literature does not justify assuming that every criticism of tone is racially motivated. It does justify examining whether supposedly neutral judgments of professionalism are behaviorally anchored, consistently applied, and auditable across race and gender.

## 8. Emotional Restriction and the Performance of Safety

If a person expects their anger to be overperceived or punished, emotional self-monitoring becomes rational.

Black men may respond by speaking more softly, smiling more, reducing gestures, avoiding disagreement, changing clothing, managing posture, creating physical distance, overexplaining intent, suppressing frustration, or leaving situations that feel likely to escalate. These strategies can be understood as forms of **anticipatory threat management**: behavior designed not because the individual is dangerous, but because the individual expects to be read as dangerous.

Ethnographic research complicates the picture in useful ways. Jackson (2013), studying Black college men, found that Black men did not simply avoid anger; in some contexts, group leaders strategically displayed anger to encourage what they understood as respectable masculine behavior and to challenge conduct they believed reinforced harmful stereotypes. The finding is important because it rejects a simplistic model in which Black men are only passive recipients of stereotype pressure. They also interpret, resist, negotiate, and sometimes strategically deploy emotion.

The broader point is that racialized emotion norms can create **emotional labor**. The Black man is not only managing what he feels. He may also be managing how another person is likely to racialize what he feels.

That is a different task.

---

## 9. Vulnerability Denied: Pain, Care, and the Myth of Invulnerability

The threatening Black man and the invulnerable Black man are not opposites. They can be two sides of the same schema.

Research on racial bias in pain perception has shown that Black people can be assumed to feel less pain than White people. Hoffman and colleagues (2016) found that false beliefs about biological differences between Black and White people were associated with racial bias in pain perception and with less accurate pain-treatment recommendations among White medical students and residents. Trawalter, Hoffman, and Waytz (2012) likewise reported evidence that participants assumed Black people felt less pain than White people.

These studies are not exclusively about Black men, and they should not be misrepresented as male-specific effects. Their relevance is conceptual: a person culturally coded as physically formidable or unusually tough may also be granted less vulnerability.

For Black men, this can interact with masculine norms in a particularly difficult way. The person presumed dangerous when assertive may also be presumed resilient when injured. Anger may be read as threat; pain may be read as tolerable; fear may be socially unavailable; vulnerability may be discounted.

The trope therefore narrows not only the space for anger, but the space for recognized **human fragility**.

## 10. Psychological Cost: Discrimination as Repeated Exposure

The strongest version of this paper’s argument is not that a stereotype directly causes a specific psychiatric outcome. The evidence does not support such a simple causal chain. A more defensible claim is that repeated racial discrimination constitutes a meaningful psychosocial stressor, and Black men’s exposure to discrimination is associated with poorer mental-health outcomes.

Britt-Spells and colleagues (2018) conducted a meta-analysis focused specifically on Black men in the United States. Across the literature they reviewed, perceived racial discrimination was positively associated with depressive symptoms, with a pooled correlation of approximately *r* = .29.

That relationship provides a health context for the daily interpretive burden described throughout this paper. If a man repeatedly expects his motives, body, tone, or emotion to be read through a threat frame, the cost is not limited to the individual encounter. The anticipation of future encounters can itself shape behavior, vigilance, coping, and well-being.

The concept of **accumulation** is therefore essential. A single biased interaction may be ambiguous. A lifetime of managing the possibility of biased interpretation is a different phenomenon.

## 11. A Proposed Framework: The Racialized Anger-Threat Loop

To organize the literature, this paper proposes the **Racialized Anger-Threat Loop (RATL)**. The framework is conceptual and has not yet been validated as a unified empirical model. It is offered as a testable synthesis.

### Stage 1 — Presumption

The observer carries cultural or learned associations linking Black men with anger, hostility, danger, criminality, toughness, or physical dominance.

### Stage 2 — Ambiguous cue

The target presents a cue whose meaning is not self-evident: neutral facial affect, direct eye contact, silence, disagreement, rapid movement, physical size, frustration, confidence, defensive speech, or actual anger.

### Stage 3 — Amplification

The cue is interpreted through the prior. Anger is detected earlier; size or strength is overestimated; hostility is inferred; innocence is discounted; vulnerability is underrecognized.

### Stage 4 — Penalty or control response

The changed interpretation alters behavior toward the target: social distancing, loss of influence, discipline, exclusion, surveillance, denial of opportunity, escalation, force, or discounting of pain.

### Stage 5 — Adaptation

The Black male target adjusts behavior in response to expected interpretation: self-silencing, emotional suppression, strategic friendliness, code switching, avoidance, hypervigilance, or deliberate performance of respectability or nonthreatening affect.

### Stage 6 — Confirmation error

The adaptation itself can be reinterpreted. Silence may be called suspicious. Confidence may be called arrogance. Controlled emotion may be called coldness. Frustration after repeated scrutiny may be taken as proof of the original stereotype.

### Stage 7 — Accumulation

Repeated exposure changes opportunity structures and psychological burden over time. What began as a momentary perceptual bias becomes consequential through repetition and institutional decision making.

The loop predicts an especially important asymmetry: **the Black man may have to disconfirm threat repeatedly, while the observer may need only one ambiguous cue to confirm it.**

That prediction is empirically testable and should be a priority for future research.

---

## 12. What This Framework Does Not Claim

A credible theory must specify its boundaries.

First, the framework does **not** claim that Black men are never angry, aggressive, violent, rude, mistaken, or responsible for harmful conduct. Black men have the same human capacity for constructive and destructive behavior as anyone else. Equal humanity requires rejecting both demonization and romanticization.

Second, it does not claim that every negative interaction involving a Black man is caused by racism. Specific events require specific evidence.

Third, it does not claim that implicit bias explains all racial disparities in policing, employment, education, or health. Structural conditions, law, organizational incentives, neighborhood context, socioeconomic inequality, explicit discrimination, historical policy, and individual behavior also matter.

Fourth, the framework should not erase Black women’s distinct experiences. The “Angry Black Woman” stereotype has its own intersectional history and empirical literature. Black men and Black women may both face racialized anger stereotypes while experiencing different gendered expectations and penalties.

Fifth, not every study cited here is nationally representative, and laboratory findings do not automatically reproduce with identical magnitude in field settings. Some studies use convenience samples, hypothetical scenarios, or specific target populations. External validity must be assessed rather than assumed.

These limitations do not nullify the evidence. They define what the evidence can responsibly support.

## 13. Institutional Design: Reducing the Power of Ambiguous Impressions

If the problem were merely personal prejudice, institutions could respond with awareness campaigns alone. The research suggests a stronger approach: redesign decisions so that ambiguous impressions have less unchecked power.

### 13.1 Replace impression labels with observable behavior

Terms such as “aggressive,” “hostile,” “not a culture fit,” “intimidating,” or “bad attitude” should trigger a second question: **What specifically happened?**

A behaviorally anchored record is more auditable than a personality label. “Raised his voice after being interrupted three times” is different from “aggressive.” “Declined the request and cited policy” is different from “uncooperative.”

### 13.2 Use structured criteria before the encounter

Hiring, discipline, promotion, school sanctions, security escalation, and clinical triage should rely as much as possible on criteria defined before decision makers encounter the individual case. Precommitment reduces the opportunity for a racialized impression to quietly become a new standard after the fact.

### 13.3 Separate emotion from substance

An angry delivery does not make a factual claim wrong. Institutions should train decision makers to evaluate at least two dimensions separately:

- Was the conduct itself outside an established behavioral standard?
- Was the underlying claim, complaint, or evidence valid?

A person can communicate imperfectly and still be substantively correct.

### 13.4 Audit outcomes at the intersection of race and gender

Aggregate racial statistics can conceal gender-specific patterns; aggregate gender statistics can conceal racial patterns. Organizations should examine outcomes by race and gender together when legally and ethically appropriate: discipline, promotion, performance ratings, use of security, customer complaints, escalation, pain management, and other high-discretion decisions.

### 13.5 Build review into high-stakes subjective judgments

The higher the cost of a subjective label, the stronger the review process should be. A label such as “threatening” that affects termination, expulsion, restraint, or force should require contemporaneous behavioral evidence and, where feasible, independent review.

### 13.6 Measure whether interventions change behavior, not only attitudes

Bias training is insufficient if people leave with improved vocabulary but unchanged decisions. Institutions should measure downstream behavior: Did discipline gaps narrow? Did performance feedback become more behaviorally specific? Did escalation rates change? Did clinical recommendations become more consistent?

The objective is not to prove that every individual is unbiased. The objective is to build systems that remain fairer even when human perception is imperfect.

## 14. Research Agenda

The literature supports the components of the proposed framework more strongly than it supports the framework as a unified causal chain. Future research should test the chain directly.

### 14.1 Neutral-to-angry thresholds

Experiments should estimate whether identical Black and White male targets require different objective levels of facial or vocal anger before observers classify them as angry, hostile, or threatening.

### 14.2 Anger versus assertiveness

Researchers should distinguish anger from confident disagreement. Many real-world conflicts involve directness without anger. A key question is whether Black male assertiveness is more likely to be recoded as anger.

### 14.3 Consequence mapping

Studies should connect perception to actual decisions: hiring, promotion, discipline, security intervention, medical triage, customer-service escalation, or credibility judgments.

### 14.4 Repeated-interaction studies

Most experiments capture snapshots. The lived burden may arise from repetition. Longitudinal research should examine the cumulative cognitive and physiological effects of repeated emotional self-monitoring.

### 14.5 Within-group heterogeneity

Skin tone, facial prototypicality, body size, age, class signals, accent, hairstyle, disability, and context may change the effect. Treating all Black men as interchangeable would reproduce the same flattening this paper criticizes.

### 14.6 Protective factors and resistance

Research should study not only harm but also agency: supportive supervisors, race-concordant mentorship, procedural transparency, collective advocacy, emotional authenticity, social support, and institutional practices that allow Black men to disagree or express emotion without automatic threat escalation.

## 15. Discussion

The evidence reviewed here supports a reframing of the “Angry Black Man.” The stereotype is not best understood as a single proposition about temper. It is an **interpretive system** that can alter the meaning assigned to Black male presence and emotion.

The system can begin with adultification: the Black boy receives less presumption of innocence. It can continue through emotion perception: anger is more readily detected in Black faces under racial bias. It can become embodied: the Black man is perceived as larger or more formidable than an equivalent White man. In professional settings, anger can diminish influence even when the argument is held constant. In healthcare, adjacent beliefs about toughness and pain tolerance can make vulnerability less visible. Across repeated experiences, perceived racial discrimination is associated with depressive symptoms among Black men.

The through-line is not that every institution acts from the same motive. The through-line is that **ambiguity is not distributed equally when stereotypes supply different default interpretations.**

This has a practical implication. Telling Black men simply to “control their anger” accepts the stereotype’s frame and locates the entire solution inside the person being perceived. Some situations do require any person to regulate anger. But regulation cannot solve a system in which neutral behavior can already be interpreted as angry, physical presence can already be inflated into threat, and justified anger can already reduce credibility.

A fairer society must widen the range of Black male behavior that is permitted to remain ordinary: quiet without suspiciousness, confident without arrogance, firm without menace, frustrated without dangerousness, vulnerable without weakness, and angry without automatic dehumanization.

The objective is not special permission for anger. It is equal interpretive latitude.

---

## 16. Conclusion

The Angry Black Man trope is socially powerful precisely because it can transform interpretation before anyone explicitly invokes race. It can turn neutrality into hostility, size into danger, confidence into aggression, pain into toughness, disagreement into insubordination, and emotional expression into evidence against the person expressing it.

The empirical literature does not support reducing Black men’s lives to victimhood, nor does it support a monocausal account of racial inequality. It does support a more disciplined conclusion: across multiple domains, researchers have documented racialized biases in the perception of Black male innocence, anger, threat, formidability, and social influence, while broader discrimination is associated with meaningful economic and psychological costs.

A useful next step is therefore not another argument over whether the trope “exists” in the abstract. The better questions are measurable:

- At what point does an observer decide a Black man is angry?
- What behavior would be interpreted differently if the same person were White?
- Which institutional decisions are most vulnerable to ambiguous threat judgments?
- What procedural safeguards reduce those differences?
- What is the cumulative cost to Black men of continually managing other people’s interpretations?

Those questions move the discussion from stereotype as rhetoric to stereotype as a testable system of perception and consequence.
